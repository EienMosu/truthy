Taxonomy: frontend > Web platform > topics > Web security. There is no exam guide; the OWASP Top 10:2025 (the current released edition), MDN Web Docs and the W3C, WHATWG and IETF specifications are the source of truth. The audience is working frontend developers.

r8 Content Security Policy (fetch directives and the default-src fallback, nonces and hashes, 'strict-dynamic' and a strict CSP, 'unsafe-inline' and 'unsafe-eval' and when 'unsafe-inline' is ignored, Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only, report-to with Reporting-Endpoints and the deprecated report-uri, delivery by header versus the meta element, require-trusted-types-for)
r9 Cross-site request forgery (how CSRF works, CSRF tokens, Fetch metadata headers such as Sec-Fetch-Site, requests that need a preflight such as those with a custom header or a JSON Content-Type as a defence for fetch() callers, which defence fits form submissions versus JavaScript requests, SameSite cookies as defence in depth)
r10 Clickjacking and framing control (how clickjacking works, the frame-ancestors directive and its values, X-Frame-Options DENY and SAMEORIGIN, the obsolete ALLOW-FROM, which mechanisms have no effect in a meta element, frame-ancestors having no default-src fallback, SameSite cookies as an additional partial mitigation)
r11 Subresource Integrity (the integrity attribute, the sha256, sha384 and sha512 prefixes, multiple hashes and the strongest-algorithm rule, the crossorigin attribute for cross-origin resources, what the browser does on a mismatch, which elements and link rel values support it)
r12 HTTPS, HSTS, mixed content and Referrer Policy (secure contexts, upgradable versus blockable mixed content, Strict-Transport-Security max-age, includeSubDomains and preload, the header being ignored over HTTP, the first-visit weakness and the preload list, Referrer-Policy values, the default strict-origin-when-cross-origin and the earlier no-referrer-when-downgrade)

RULES FOR THE STATEMENT
- Original wording. Do not copy or paraphrase practice exams, exam dumps or course material.
- One fact per statement. No compound statements where one half is true and the other false.
- Maximum 120 characters. Plain declarative sentence, present tense.
- No negations ("is not", "cannot", "never", "without") in the statement.
- No absolutes ("always", "all", "only", "every", "must") unless the official source states it that way.
- Use official product and API names exactly as the official source writes them today.
- A false statement is false for exactly one clear reason, and that reason is a realistic misconception a learner who studied could hold. Never make a statement false through trick wording, spelling or a changed number.
- No strawman: if nobody who studied would answer True, write a different card.
- No truism: a statement that is true by common sense, by definition or by arithmetic, and that is not specific to this subject, teaches nothing. Write a different card.
- No name giveaway: the answer must not be readable from the product name alone (for example "X for Windows File Server provides Windows file systems").
- True and false statements must be indistinguishable by form: within each section keep the average length of the false statements within 5 characters of the average length of the true ones, and use the same sentence patterns for both.
- No wording tells. A player who does not know the subject must not be able to guess from the words. Hedge verbs ("can", "supports", "lets", "allows", "may") must not mark true statements: use them about as often in false statements as in true ones. Requirement and coverage words ("requires", "must", "each", "every", "automatically", "by default", "directly") must not mark false statements: use them about as often in true statements as in false ones. Count them across the deck before every answer and rebalance.
- No recurring false-statement template, such as "X requires Y" where Y is optional, or "each X has its own Y": a template may carry at most two false statements in the whole deck.
- Write in your own words; do not reproduce a sentence of the official page with a word or two changed.
- Name the actor. No passive sentence whose answer depends on who does something ("a datacenter is selected" must say who selects it).
- The two sides of one distinction are one fact. If a card tests that A is X, no other card may test that B, the other side of the same two-way split, is Y (for example CapEx versus OpEx, horizontal versus vertical scaling).
- No strawman and no definition card: a statement whose answer follows from the meaning of a term in it (elastic, serverless, open standard, streaming) teaches nothing.
- Do not rely on facts that change often: prices, exact quotas and limits, counts of regions, free tier amounts, launch dates, preview features. If a card depends on something that could change within a year or two, set "volatility" to "may_change" and say why in "volatilityNote". Otherwise "stable".
- Leave out products that are deprecated, in maintenance mode or closed to new customers.
- Difficulty mix within each section: roughly 40% easy (1), 40% medium (2), 20% hard (3). Hard means a real distinction between two similar things, not an obscure detail.
- Depth: a frontend developer who ships web apps and owns their headers, cookies and client code. Test mechanisms and their limits: what a header, attribute or directive does, where it must be delivered, what a browser blocks or allows, which attack a defence stops and which it does not. No trivia about CWE numbers, CWE counts per category, incidence percentages or the history of the list.
- Pin the OWASP edition. Every card in r1 to r3 names the edition inside the statement ("In the OWASP Top 10:2025, ...") and uses the 2025 category IDs and names exactly as https://top10.owasp.org/2025/ writes them. Known traps: the 2021 names "Vulnerable and Outdated Components", "Identification and Authentication Failures" and "Security Logging and Monitoring Failures" are replaced in 2025; A08 is "Software or Data Integrity Failures" in 2025 (2021 wrote "Software and Data Integrity Failures"), a change the Introduction does not announce, so do not build a card on that one-word difference; Server-Side Request Forgery is no longer a separate category but part of A01:2025; Cross-site Scripting sits under A05:2025 Injection; Cryptographic Failures is A04, Security Misconfiguration is A02. The Introduction calls A03 both new and "an expansion of A06:2021": word cards about A03 the way the Introduction does. A card that tests a 2021 name must make the 2021 edition explicit and is limited to the what-changed facts the 2025 Introduction states.
- r1 to r3 test OWASP's own framing (which category covers a given weakness, what its "How to prevent" guidance says, the 2025 changes); the mechanism of XSS, CSRF, CORS, cookies and HSTS belongs to r4 to r12. Do not test the same fact once as an OWASP mapping and once as a browser mechanism.
- Never state a judgement as a fact. "X is the best defence" is acceptable only when MDN or OWASP says so in those terms (for example MDN calls a nonce "the recommended approach" for restricting the loading of script and style resources). Prefer statements about behaviour.
- Browser-dependent behaviour must be worded as the reference page words it, and set "volatility" to "may_change" for anything about browser support, defaults that differ between browsers, third-party cookies or the HSTS preload list. Specific traps: (a) SameSite default: the Set-Cookie reference says "Some browsers use Lax as the default value if SameSite is not specified" (MDN compatibility data lists this for Chrome, behind a preference in Firefox, not in Safari), while the MDN Cookies guide says a cookie without SameSite "is treated as Lax by default"; follow the Set-Cookie reference and never claim that every browser defaults to Lax; the two-minute POST exception is browser behaviour and "may_change". (b) SameSite=None: state the rule as MDN does ("The Secure attribute must also be set") without claiming that every browser rejects a cookie that breaks it. (c) Third-party cookies: the MDN Third-party cookies page says Chrome does not block them by default while Firefox and Safari restrict them by default; the Cookies guide's claim that vendors "have all started to block third-party cookies by default" is not a safe basis for a card, and do not test Chrome's past deprecation plans or Privacy Sandbox. (d) Trusted Types: the API page shows Baseline 2026 Newly available (since February 2026) while the MDN XSS guide still says it "does not yet have good cross-browser support"; cards about its availability say "Baseline" rather than "every browser" and are "may_change". (e) Partitioned cookies are recent in Safari; cards about their availability are "may_change". (f) Schemeful same-site is applied by some browsers only; cards say "schemeful same-site" explicitly instead of claiming every browser treats http and https as different sites.
- Name and status traps: X-XSS-Protection is deprecated and non-standard, and MDN warns it can create XSS vulnerabilities (do not present it as a defence); X-Frame-Options ALLOW-FROM is obsolete and browsers that meet it ignore the whole header; X-Frame-Options, frame-ancestors and Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only have no effect in a meta element; frame-ancestors does not fall back to default-src; report-uri is deprecated in favour of report-to, and browsers that support report-to ignore report-uri; the document.domain setter is deprecated; the Referrer-Policy default is strict-origin-when-cross-origin (previously no-referrer-when-downgrade); mixed content is now described as "upgradable" and "blockable" (formerly passive or optionally blockable, and active), and browsers upgrade upgradable content to HTTPS rather than loading it over HTTP; the HSTS preload directive is not part of RFC 6797 but a convention of the preload list service; "simple request" is a term from the obsolete CORS specification that the Fetch Standard does not use, so cards say "a request that does not trigger a preflight" or name the conditions. Leave out the __Http- and __Host-Http- cookie prefixes (defined in the rfc6265bis draft, not supported in Safari per MDN compatibility data) and anything a source marks experimental or non-standard, except to state that something is deprecated.
- XSS terminology: OWASP names reflected, stored and DOM based XSS and the newer server XSS and client XSS terms; MDN's XSS guide uses client-side and server-side XSS and does not use the word "reflected". A card about a type uses the source's own terms and cites that source.
- No numeric limits beyond those stated as rules in the sources (for example the preload requirement of a max-age of at least 31536000 seconds with includeSubDomains is allowed; the TLS version OWASP recommends is allowed only as OWASP words it).
- Overlap with other decks: the game has Next.js Rendering, and React Fundamentals, TypeScript Fundamentals, GitHub Actions and Docker Fundamentals are written in parallel. Do not test framework or tool APIs here except at most one card on a framework escape hatch such as React's dangerouslySetInnerHTML in r7 (MDN names it), and no Next.js configuration. Supply chain cards in r2 stay at OWASP's level (dependencies, SBOM, build servers and CI/CD, distribution) and do not test features of any CI product or package manager.
